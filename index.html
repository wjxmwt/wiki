PAH. FROM THE ENVIRONMENT TO THE LAB

01On the road

Emissions on the road

A car passes. Behind it, exhaust spreads into the air above the road.

Fuel combustion can release polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, or PAHs. Some pollutants remain after the visible exhaust has dispersed.

Some PAHs attach to airborne particles. Air can carry those particles beyond the road where they began.

02Industrial emissions

Above the industrial district

Road traffic is one source of PAHs. Industrial combustion is another.

Smoke spreads above the chimneys. Its appearance alone cannot tell us which pollutants are present, or how much there is.

Particles travel with the air before some settle onto the ground. This is one way pollutants reach the soil.

03Deposition

Pollutants settle in soil

A particle lands beside a young plant. In the soil below, roots grow between grains of earth and small pores.

Some PAHs bind to soil particles and can persist there. Their distribution depends on the soil and the compounds involved.

Rain wets the surface. As water begins to flow, some loose particles move with it; others stay behind.

04Surface runoff

Rain carries particles downstream

Small streams of rainwater meet along the slope and flow into a drainage channel.

The water carries soil and suspended particles. Some of those particles may also carry PAHs.

At the outlet, runoff enters the river. This movement transfers part of the pollution; it does not mean the pollutants have disappeared.

05Water sampling

Taking a water sample

At the riverbank, a sample bottle enters the water. The illustration follows a sample from collection to the laboratory.

The bottle fills and is lifted from the river. Clear-looking water can still contain substances we cannot identify by eye.

The sample is labelled before it reaches the bench. A record of where and how it was collected helps later analysis.

06In the laboratory

From the river to the lab

The bottle is set on the bench. Small volumes of liquid can now be transferred and studied under controlled conditions.

A pipette takes up a small volume and releases it into an observation area.

Our project studies a metabolic pathway and a sensing system. To explain their connection, we follow naphthalene, one member of the PAH family.

07Naphthalene metabolism

Following naphthalene

Naphthalene has two connected carbon rings. It is the starting compound in the pathway shown here.

Several enzyme-catalysed reactions convert naphthalene into salicylate. This is a sequence of chemical changes, not a single instantaneous step.

Salicylate is the compound recognised by our sensing system. Following it connects the metabolic pathway to the fluorescent readout.

08Fluorescence

Reading the fluorescent signal

Salicylate binds to the regulatory protein NahR. This interaction enables the next stage of the response.

Activated NahR promotes expression from Psal. Transcription and translation lead to production of the fluorescent protein eGFP.

With suitable excitation light, eGFP produces green fluorescence. Measurements and controls are needed to interpret that signal.

09Our project

Explore the research

The view returns to the laboratory. A sample, a record and a signal each tell us something different.

Our project investigates PAH detection and degradation. Experiments and models help us examine the steps connecting the two.

Explore the experiments, follow the models, or meet the team carrying out this work.
